## Abstract

Unswell is a linter for English prose in documentation. It started as a set of rules for formulaic wording and grew, by trial and error, into three tools: rules that report a construction, an index that says how much a page reads like a model's text, and a check of what a revision lost.

This report covers the work from September 7 to November 18, 2026. Most of what I tried did not pass the test I had set for it beforehand. Statistical detectors of generated text did not transfer to technical prose. Learned selectors of sentences did not beat a word list. Rules that looked precise fired as often on sentences that editors left alone as on those they changed.

Three things held. An index of six typographic and lexical marks, with a second reading of sentence rhythm, tells the pages before a cleanup commit from the pages of people. A fix that needs no model lowered that index on every page of its test. And a check of a revision caught every damaged copy of a page that I gave it.

The main result is a limit. A model that revises a post by the tool's report takes out what the rules name, and blind judges still pick the post as the model's text about as often as before. The rules name habits that a reader notices. Removing them does not make a text read as a person's.

<Note>
Every annotator, reviewer, and judge in this report is a language model. No number here is a human judgment, except where the text names professional writers or editors of a public data set. I accepted that for this work in [ADR 0041](https://github.com/stokaro/unswell/blob/main/docs/adr/0041-assistant-review-acceptance.md).
</Note>

## The question

The typical reader of a finding already knows where the text came from. They asked a model for a README, and they want the stock phrases and the rhetorical templates pointed at, each with a reason. Their question is what to rewrite.

That was not clear from the start. The first weeks mixed two aims: naming constructions worth rewriting, and telling generated text from human text. [ADR 0037](https://github.com/stokaro/unswell/blob/main/docs/adr/0037-diagnostics-not-authorship.md) separated them on September 11. Unswell gives style diagnostics. An estimate of origin can exist as research, off by default, and it can never fail a build.

The second aim came back in a narrower form in November, as an index of how much a page *sounds* like a model's. That index says nothing about who wrote a page, and a person who writes in short even sentences gets the same number.

## Materials

The measurements used six kinds of text.

- **Labeled pages.** A model annotated 123 editorial events on 36 documentation pages. For six weeks this was the screen that every new method had to pass.
- **The panel corpus.** Seven sets of generated and real documentation pages, 3,795 documents in all, with 11,322 findings that two models from different families reviewed ([the second report](https://github.com/stokaro/unswell/blob/main/research/report-v2.md)).
- **Edits by people.** Paragraphs that professional writers edited, from the Beemo and LAMP data sets, and documentation that maintainers reworded in pull requests.
- **Cleanup commits.** Files before and after a commit whose message says that it removes AI-sounding wording: 959 pairs from 648 repositories for fitting and 597 pairs from 423 for the test ([ADR 0056](https://github.com/stokaro/unswell/blob/main/docs/adr/0056-rules-tested-on-de-slop-commits.md)).
- **Text under a style guide.** Posts of one product blog that a model wrote under the blog's style guide, and texts that two models wrote from the outlines of human posts, with and without a style guide.
- **Pages of people.** Blog posts, articles written under a style guide, and documentation pages, all from before November 2022.

No record in the repository holds the text of a page. A record keeps counts, hashes, and the scripts that made them.

## How a result is admitted

Four habits decide what counts as a result here. They came out of my mistakes, mostly of the kind where a number looked good on the pages that had shaped the method.

**The bar comes first.** A threshold is written on the issue before the run that it judges. A run that misses it is recorded as it is.

**Half of the pages are held back.** A hash assigns each page to a fitting half or to a measured half. Forms, weights, and limits are chosen on the first. The bars are read on the second.

**A record recounts itself.** A record carries a program that recomputes every number of its results from the data beside it, and the repository's check runs those programs.

**The published binary is tried again.** After a release, the binary from the release page is run on the same texts and has to give the same rows.

### September: an engine, and a question about origin

The first alpha shipped on the day of the first commit: a Go library, a command, and an extractor that finds prose in Markdown, comments, and string literals. Within a week the project also had a statistical side, with features, calibration, and a channel for estimates of origin.

The first report closed that line of work. Two frozen hypotheses about rules did not replicate. An effect of +2.96 points in development became +0.41 points on text that the screening had not seen ([the first report](https://github.com/stokaro/unswell/blob/main/research/report-v1.md)). The one signal that survived was lexical. It separated generated documentation from human code comments at recall 0.875 and a false-flag rate of 0.052, after three attempts on prepared features that turned out to measure length.

What a reader receives from the catalog, that report found, follows the length of the text, then its kind, then the model that wrote it. Whether a model wrote it comes last.

### September to November: recall on labeled pages

The linter was quiet on real pages. So the goal became recall: of the 123 events that a reviewer had marked, how many does a method find, and how many of its findings does the reviewer accept? The bar was 80% of the events and 85% of the findings.

Contextual rules helped a little. Learned selectors of sentences, from small encoders to fitted classifiers, stayed below a plain lexical baseline. Pipelines that asked a model to audit a whole page recognized more events and lost others. One recognized 55 of 123 events and lost 44 that had been recognized before ([ADR 0044](https://github.com/stokaro/unswell/blob/main/docs/adr/0044-joint-wording-audit-rejected.md)). Another raised recall from 96 to 104 of the 123 events, but in the cohort of whole documents only 77.4% of its criticisms were useful ([ADR 0045](https://github.com/stokaro/unswell/blob/main/docs/adr/0045-separated-admission-rejected.md)).

In the same weeks I read the literature on detecting generated text and tried what could run offline: my own simple measures on public data sets, and published detectors on the project's kinds of text. Table 1 has six of those screens. Each had a rule written before the run.

*Table 1. Six screens of signals of origin, each against the rule that was registered for it. The first three test my own measures on public data. The last three test published detectors.*

The published detectors may well do what their papers report on the benchmarks of those papers. On rewrites of a person's text and on technical documentation that a model has polished, neither they nor my own measures reached the floors that would let a linter act on them.

By November 11 more than 60 frozen pilots had failed the screen of 123 events, and the engine reached 45 of them. [ADR 0046](https://github.com/stokaro/unswell/blob/main/docs/adr/0046-assistant-panel-corpus.md) gave three reasons. The reference was too small to learn from. It was exposed, since every hypothesis had its one run on the same pages. And the corpus that the plan had called for, with labels by two human raters, did not exist.

### November 11 to 13: what editors change

The screen was retired, and the question was turned around. Instead of asking a method to find marked events, two reviewers from different model families judged what the engine reported.

That showed why the linter had been quiet. Phrase rules had shipped with a handful of examples, and many rules were off because nobody had measured them. A fitted word model did not carry over either: with repositories held out it reached 18.7% recall at 85% precision on generated documents, and no recall at that precision on 164 real pages ([ADR 0047](https://github.com/stokaro/unswell/blob/main/docs/adr/0047-qualified-defaults-and-assistant-review.md)). The two reviewers agreed on which sentences need revision with a kappa of 0.43.

Then came the comparison with people, and it was sobering ([ADR 0052](https://github.com/stokaro/unswell/blob/main/docs/adr/0052-claims-follow-human-references.md)). Three professional writers who edited the same paragraphs agree on which words to change with a kappa of 0.11. On documentation that maintainers reworded, the default rules flag 4.0% of the reworded sentences and 4.2% of the others. A rule set that fires as often on kept sentences as on reworded ones cannot claim to find what an editor would change, and I stopped claiming it.

A second look at pages of people gave the defaults their limit. On 121 documentation pages from before November 2022, 61 had a finding of a default rule. A rule now ships on only if it reports on at most one in twenty such pages, and six rules were turned off ([ADR 0058](https://github.com/stokaro/unswell/blob/main/docs/adr/0058-default-rules-quiet-on-human-pages.md)).

### November 14 and 15: an index of a page

Single findings had failed as a measure, so the next attempt looked at the page as a whole. People who clean a README of AI-sounding wording remove a short list of things: em dashes, arrows, emoji in headings and at list items, bold labels in lists, and stock words. The index counts six such marks and gives a page a number from 0 to 100.

On held-out pairs it does what it was built for. It separates pages before a cleanup commit from README files of people with an area under the curve of 0.87, and it puts 18 of 566 of those README files at its highest level. `unswell fix` replaces the marks without a model. On 83 pages it lowered the index on all 83.

Then the index met text that it could not read. A model that writes under a style guide leaves almost none of the six marks. On 76 posts of one product blog, written by a model under the blog's style guide, the index gave 71 of the posts its lowest level ([ADR 0060](https://github.com/stokaro/unswell/blob/main/docs/adr/0060-rhythm-reading.md)).

What gives such a post away is its rhythm: run after run of plain statements of similar length. The index got a second reading that counts those runs.

<Figure id="separation" number={4}>
How well the index tells texts written by a model from blog posts of people, on the measured half. Half is chance, and a value under it means that the posts of people carried more of the marks. With the rhythm reading every set is told apart, and 14 of 352 pages of people are at the highest level.
</Figure>

### November 15 to 18: checking a revision

An index invites a shortcut: revise until the number falls. It falls after almost any edit of a run of sentences. A report that asked for longer sentences took the median index of 40 posts from 69 to 23, while three judges found the revision more like a model's text than the original on 24 of the 40 ([ADR 0061](https://github.com/stokaro/unswell/blob/main/docs/adr/0061-blind-check-of-a-revision.md)). A writer who watched the index would have kept those revisions.

So the product got a command that looks at a revision without the index. `unswell compare` matches the sentences of a page with those of its revision, sends every changed sentence to a judge, names the words that were left out, and asks the judge blind which of the two texts reads more like a model's.

Two findings shaped it. The blind answer does not repeat: on posts, a "better" from one run came again 3 times of 8 with one judge and 0 of 3 with the other ([ADR 0066](https://github.com/stokaro/unswell/blob/main/docs/adr/0066-two-results-of-compare.md)). The result on what was lost does repeat, on 55 of 57 revisions with one judge and 49 of 57 with the other. The command now reports the two apart and lets a caller require the stable one alone.

Below: revisions that two models made, read by both judges. The model that revised decides more than the judge that reads, and each judge is a little kinder to revisions of its own family.
</Figure>

The last rules came from one post. I read it and named what gives it away, and two of those things could be counted: paragraph after paragraph ends by saying what something is not, and the page never leaves one formal register. Both rules report a page once.

### The result that did not go my way

The goal for the last week was plain: after a revision by the tool's report, a post should not be told from an article that a person wrote. The bar said that a panel of three judges may pick the revised post as the model's text in at most 55% of cases.

It did not hold. Against blog posts of people, the panel picked 22 of 30 posts as they were published, and 22 of 30 after a revision by either model ([record](https://github.com/stokaro/unswell/blob/main/research/reviews/2026-11-15-revision-check/RESULTS.md)).

Three pilots on the fitting half then tried harder ([record](https://github.com/stokaro/unswell/blob/main/research/reviews/2026-11-17-revision-pilots/README.md)). In the first, a model got the report and the list of closing sentences, and took them out: 98 such sentences became 4. In the second it rewrote each post in the manner of two human posts. In the third, six posts went through four rounds in which the strictest judge said why it took the post for a model's and the reviser got those remarks. That judge named the post in all 60 of its judgments.

<Figure id="revision" number={7}>
The first pilot, on 38 posts. Above: what the product reads. Below: how many of the posts each judge picks as the model's text, against posts of people. The index falls by two thirds and the judges barely move.
</Figure>

One of the three judges explains part of this. It named the model's text in 147 of 147 pairs in which two models had written under a style guide from the outline of a human post. Whatever that judge reads lies beyond the habits that the rules name.

## What held and what did not

| Tried | Outcome |
| --- | --- |
| Statistical detection of generated text | Did not transfer to technical prose. Kept as research, off by default. |
| Recall on a small set of labeled pages | More than 60 pilots failed. The screen was retired. |
| A fitted word model, sentence embeddings | No recall on real pages. Stopped. |
| Rules as a stand-in for an editor | At chance against what editors changed. The claim was withdrawn. |
| Rules that ship on by default | Kept under a limit of one page of people in twenty. |
| An index of a page, with a fix | Held on pages that no fitting had seen. Shipped. |
| A blind verdict as a gate | Does not repeat between runs. Reported, and no longer required. |
| A check of what a revision lost | Catches every damaged copy. Shipped. |
| A revision that reads as a person's | Not reached by any revision that a model made. |

## What I learned about measuring

**A number that moves is not yet a result.** The index fell after almost any edit. Only a comparison that did not use the index showed that some of those edits made the text worse.

**A judge has to be asked twice.** A blind verdict that comes out differently on the next run cannot gate a cleanup of many pages. I found that out because two records had run the same revisions twice.

**A judge is kinder to its own family.** Each of the two judges found nothing lost more often in revisions by its own family of models. The check warns against such a pairing, and the measurement shows why.

**Held-back pages get used up.** A changed rule that is run again on the measured half has seen those pages once already. The record of the last rule says so, and two of its bars held at their limit.

**Small references mislead.** A screen of 123 events absorbed six weeks. Every method had one run on the same 36 pages, so a failure said as much about those pages as about the method.

## Limits

The rules and the index read English. The judges are models, and where people's judgments exist (three writers on one set of paragraphs) they agree with each other only weakly. The pages are blog posts, articles, and documentation; reference pages and specifications, which people often write in one register on purpose, are outside every measurement.

The posts of the product blog come from one blog and one style guide. The two habits that the last rules name were found on them, and the denials did not show up in the texts of the two other models.

Unswell is an alpha. Its rules and defaults still change with what the next measurement finds.
